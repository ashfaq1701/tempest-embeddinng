# Early val peak → dip → slow second rise, with monotone train loss: mechanisms, fixes, diagnostics

## Summary (read this first)

The shape you describe — held-out metric peaks at ep1-3, loses ~10% relative over ~3 epochs, then climbs monotonically for 7+ epochs without re-reaching the peak, while train loss falls monotonically from 0.43 to 0.05 — is **epoch-wise double descent in a held-out metric**, and the three mechanisms that best fit the *specific* evidence you have are:

1. **Metric-invariant loss progress (loss–metric mismatch).** Your train loss and your val metric are not the same objective, and the near-constant `output_scale × ‖embedding‖` product is direct evidence that a large share of the loss reduction is happening along a direction a *ranking* metric is mathematically blind to (a global positive multiplicative scale on the score leaves every per-query ranking, hence MRR, exactly unchanged). Monotone train loss therefore carries almost no information about the metric here, and should not be read as "no overfitting". **Well-established** as a general phenomenon; the specific diagnosis is **plausible**.
2. **Implicit effective-learning-rate annealing along that degenerate scale direction.** When the representation norm grows and the output scale decays with a constant product, the *relative* step size on the representation falls — i.e. the run is executing an unintended LR decay schedule. That predicts exactly your two phases: an early high-effective-LR exploratory/noisy phase (the dip) followed by a low-effective-LR slowly-converging phase (the long monotone tail that patience truncates). **Well-established** for scale-invariant parameterisations; **plausible→speculative** as a transfer to an embedding table plus a learned scalar.
3. **Early easy-signal fit, then slow relational feature learning (simplicity bias / gradient starvation), with sampled-negative label noise as the amplifier.** The ep1-3 peak is the cheap signal (popularity/degree/recency) being fit almost immediately on a large one-pass dataset; the dip is over-commitment to it plus fitting of false-negative noise; the tail is the slower structural features. **Well-established** components; the composition for ranking tasks is **plausible**.

**Grokking is the weakest candidate** (needs small data, usually weight decay, and a sharp jump from chance — none of which you have). Your *second*, flatter variant (val drifts down 0.006 then rises, still below ep1) may be **no phenomenon at all**: 0.006 is within plausible epoch-to-epoch noise, and the "ep1 peak" is then a winner's-curse artefact.

**Single highest-value next experiment:** one run with (a) early stopping disabled, (b) 5× the epoch budget with a cosine decay to zero, (c) validation evaluated 4× per epoch rather than once, and (d) per-eval logging of the output scale, mean/max representation norm, and the metric recomputed after rescaling the score by a fixed constant. This one run distinguishes *truncation* (tail surpasses the early peak ⇒ double-descent/rate-limited, fix with schedule + longer training) from *irreversible early damage* (tail saturates below ⇒ memorisation/plasticity), tells you whether the "ep1-3 peak" is even a peak or just a coarsely-sampled rising curve, and confirms or kills the scale-drift story. A close second, if you can only change one thing in the model: **freeze the redundant output scale (or constrain the representation norm)** — removing the degenerate direction makes the train loss informative and is the decisive test of mechanism 2.

## 1.1 Epoch-wise double descent

**What it is.** For a fixed model, test error decreases, increases, then decreases again as training proceeds. Nakkiran et al. establish the phenomenon in both model-wise and epoch-wise form, unify them through an "effective model complexity" measure, and show that **label noise strongly amplifies it** — with clean labels the middle hump often vanishes ("Deep Double Descent: Where Bigger Models and More Data Hurt", Nakkiran, Kaplun, Bansal, Yang, Barak, Sutskever, ICLR 2020, arXiv:1912.02292). **Well-established.**

**What causes it.** Two competing published accounts, and the second is the more useful one for you:

- *Noise threshold.* Stephenson & Lee ("When and how epochwise double descent happens", arXiv:2108.12006, 2021) find epoch-wise double descent appears only once label noise crosses a critical level; below it they find no clear early-stopping point, and small/narrow networks show little or none at any noise level. They model it as the presence of **slow-to-learn but informative features**. **Plausible** (single group, ResNet18/CIFAR10).
- *Superposed bias–variance tradeoffs.* Heckel & Yilmaz ("Early Stopping in Deep Networks: Double Descent and How to Eliminate it", ICLR 2021, arXiv:2007.10099) argue the hump is **not** training-time acting as a complexity knob, but the superposition of the bias–variance tradeoffs of *different parts of the network learning at different speeds*. They prove it for linear regression and a wide two-layer net, and **remove the hump by tuning per-layer step sizes**, which also improves the early-stopped result. **Well-established** (proof + experiments, widely cited).

**Distinguishing signature.** The hump's depth and timing should scale with label-noise level; and per-part step-size rebalancing should flatten it. Crucially, in the classical form **the second descent eventually goes below the first minimum** — so "second rise never reaches the early peak" is evidence for *truncation*, not against the mechanism.

**Fit to your case.** Strong, with one caveat flagged in the gaps section: your "first descent" completes in 1-3 epochs, whereas the published curves take tens to hundreds. Your label-noise analogue is **false negatives in sampled negatives**, which is real and often large in implicit-feedback ranking (the recommender literature calls it the false-negative problem; see e.g. the recent negative-sampling papers, though I found no controlled measurement of its effect on the *shape* of the NDCG/MRR curve over epochs — **speculative** as applied here).

A very recent paper is worth reading in full for this exact shape: **"Two Speeds of Learning: A Representation-Readout Decomposition of Grokking and Double Descent"** (Chou, Uzdelewicz, Chiu, Yang, Chung; arXiv:2605.27078, NeurIPS 2026). It decomposes training into *representation learning in the encoder* vs *readout calibration in the final layer*, argues the readout is train-biased before grokking onset, and attributes apparent delayed or non-monotone generalisation to **representation degradation plus readout misalignment**. That is, almost verbatim, a formal version of your output-scale-vs-embedding-norm observation. **Plausible** (new, not replicated).

## 1.2 Early learning, then memorisation (simplicity bias / spectral bias)

**What it is.** Networks fit simple, high-signal structure first and noisy/atypical examples later. Arpit et al. ("A Closer Look at Memorization in Deep Networks", ICML 2017, PMLR 70:233-242, arXiv:1706.05394) show nets *can* memorise noise but **prioritise simple patterns first**. Rahaman et al. ("On the Spectral Bias of Neural Networks", ICML 2019, PMLR 97:5301-5310, arXiv:1806.08734) give the Fourier version: a **frequency-dependent learning speed**, low frequencies first. Kalimeris, Kaplun, Nakkiran et al. ("SGD on Neural Networks Learns Functions of Increasing Complexity", NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1905.11604) show that in the **initial epochs almost all the performance gain is explained by a linear classifier**, which is then retained throughout. Liu, Niles-Weed, Razavian, Fernandez-Granda ("Early-Learning Regularization Prevents Memorization of Noisy Labels", NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2007.00151) prove the two-phase structure in high-dimensional classification even for linear models, and build a regulariser around it. **Well-established.**

**Signature.** The peak epoch should move *earlier* as label noise increases; per-example loss trajectories bifurcate (clean examples' loss falls and stays low, noisy ones' loss falls late); a k-NN / linear probe on frozen early features should already be near the ep1-3 metric.

**Limitation — and this is the key one.** This family predicts peak-then-**monotone decline**. It does **not** predict your second rise. If you want this mechanism to explain your curve you must pair it with something that supplies the recovery (1.1, 1.3 or 1.4). Say so explicitly when you write it up.

## 1.3 Scale/norm growth, reparameterisation invariance, and effective-LR drift

This is the mechanism your *constant-product* observation points at, and it is the one most people miss.

**The three facts from the literature.**

- **Norm grows while direction converges, and loss falls forever.** For separable data and monotone losses, gradient descent's parameter norm **diverges (≈ log t)** while the normalised predictor converges to the max-margin direction — at a rate only *logarithmic* in the loss (Soudry, Hoffer, Nacson, Gunasekar, Srebro, "The Implicit Bias of Gradient Descent on Separable Data", JMLR 19(70), 2018; arXiv:1710.10345). Hoffer, Hubara, Soudry ("Train longer, generalize better", NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1705.08741) measure the same logarithmic weight-distance growth and model it as **ultra-slow diffusion**. So: **monotone train loss with a glacially slow late improvement in the metric is the predicted behaviour, not an anomaly.** **Well-established.**
- **Norm growth *is* an LR schedule.** For scale-invariant parameters the gradient scales as 1/‖w‖, so the *effective* learning rate falls as ‖w‖² grows; L2/weight decay then acts mainly by setting the weight scale and hence the effective LR, not by capacity control (van Laarhoven, "L2 Regularization versus Batch and Weight Normalization", arXiv:1706.05350, 2017 — and it notes Adam only partly removes the effect). Zhang, Wang, Xu, Grosse ("Three Mechanisms of Weight Decay Regularization", ICLR 2019, arXiv:1810.12281) confirm the effective-LR mechanism is the dominant one for SGD *and Adam* on normalised nets, and that **matching the effective LR without decay recovers the whole effect**. Li & Arora ("An Exponential Learning Rate Schedule for Deep Learning", ICLR 2020, arXiv:1910.07454) prove the equivalence formally: LR schedule + weight decay can be *folded into* a pure exponentially-growing LR, because the objective is scale-invariant. Kosson, Messmer, Jaggi ("Rotational Equilibrium: How Weight Decay Balances Learning Across Neural Networks", ICML 2024, arXiv:2305.17212) extend this to **Adam, AdamW and Lion**, using average angular rotation as the effective-LR proxy, and show explicitly controlling rotation **removes the chaotic transient phase at the start of training** and the need for warmup. **Well-established.**
- **Effective LR selects a *regime*, and the regimes have different loss behaviour.** Kodryan, Lobacheva, Nakhodnov, Vetrov ("Training Scale-Invariant Neural Networks on the Sphere Can Happen in Three Regimes", NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2209.03695) fix the effective LR and find three regimes: **convergence** (monotone loss decay), **chaotic equilibrium** (sustained oscillation), and **divergence**. They also show these regimes appear inside conventional training of normalised nets, where the effective LR drifts. **Plausible→well-established** (clean experiments, one group).

**The composite prediction for your run (my inference, marked speculative-but-testable).** You have a *degenerate* (loss-flat, metric-invariant) direction: `(output_scale ↓, representation_norm ↑)` with constant product. The optimiser sits in equilibrium along it and drifts. The consequences:
- Train loss keeps falling along directions the metric cannot see ⇒ monotone train loss is uninformative **by construction**.
- The *relative* step size on the representation shrinks as its norm grows and as the output scale (which multiplies the representation's gradient) decays ⇒ an **unintended annealing schedule**: large effective steps in ep1-6 (noisy/chaotic regime, the dip), small effective steps afterwards (converging regime, the slow tail). Patience then fires on a run whose step size has become too small to finish.
- A corollary worth stating in the paper: a global multiplicative output scale **cannot change MRR at all** (per-query rank order is invariant under a positive monotone transform of the score, and MRR aggregates per-query ranks). So any gradient budget spent on that scalar is literally wasted with respect to your metric. The condition is that the scale multiplies the *entire* score; if it multiplies only part (e.g. there is an additive popularity bias term), invariance breaks and the drift *does* move the metric — which is itself a diagnostic.

**Related caution.** Dinh, Pascanu, Bengio, Bengio ("Sharp Minima Can Generalize For Deep Nets", ICML 2017) is the standard reference for why *any* sharpness/norm-based reading of such a run is suspect: reparameterisation changes those quantities without changing the function. **Well-established.** A 2025 position paper ("Many generalization measures for deep learning are fragile", arXiv:2510.18934) makes the same point more broadly — **plausible**, I did not read it.

## 1.4 Lazy vs rich (feature-learning) regimes, catapult, edge of stability

**What it is.** Jacot, Gabriel, Hongler's NTK and the lazy-training analysis (Chizat, Oyallon, Bach, "On Lazy Training in Differentiable Programming", NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1812.07956) describe a regime where the model behaves like its linearisation at init — reaching low train loss with parameters barely moving, equivalent to kernel learning; Chizat et al. also show that **forcing real CNNs into the lazy regime degrades their performance**, i.e. the useful phase is the rich/feature-learning one. **Well-established.**

**Why this gives your shape.** A transition from lazy to rich is a transition between two *different* predictors. The lazy predictor can be decent immediately (your ep1-3 peak), the transition can transiently destroy held-out performance while the features reorganise (the dip), and the rich predictor then improves slowly (the tail). The "Two Speeds" paper above (arXiv:2605.27078) is the explicit version of this and, notably, **argues against** a pure lazy→rich account of grokking, finding representation learning gradual rather than absent. **Plausible.**

**Large-LR phases.** Lewkowycz, Bahri, Dyer, Sohl-Dickstein, Gur-Ari ("The large learning rate phase of deep learning: the catapult mechanism", arXiv:2003.02218, 2020) identify three LR phases — lazy, **catapult** (loss spikes then recovers; only the nonlinear model converges), divergent — and report that **optimal performance is often reached in the large-LR phase**. Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter, Talwalkar ("Gradient Descent on Neural Networks Typically Occurs at the Edge of Stability", ICLR 2021, arXiv:2103.00065) show full-batch GD drives the top Hessian eigenvalue to hover just above 2/η, where **train loss is non-monotone over short timescales but decreasing over long ones**. **Well-established.**

**Caveat for you.** Catapult/EoS primarily explain non-monotonicity in the *training* loss, which you do not have at epoch granularity. If your train loss is only logged once per epoch, short-timescale spikes are invisible — so EoS is **not ruled out**; it is **unmeasured**. That is a cheap thing to check (Part 3).

Kalra & Barkeshli ("Why Warmup the Learning Rate? Underlying Mechanisms and Improvements", NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2406.09405) tie this together operationally: warmup works by moving the net to better-conditioned regions, letting you use larger target LRs; they identify regimes depending on whether training begins in progressive sharpening or sharpness reduction; **and for Adam the relevant quantity is the top eigenvalue of the preconditioned Hessian**. They also propose initialising Adam's second-moment term to get warmup-like benefits. **Plausible** (recent, one group, strong analysis).

## 1.5 Grokking and delayed generalisation

Power, Burda, Edwards, Babuschkin, Misra ("Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets", arXiv:2201.02177, 2022) report networks going from chance to near-perfect generalisation long after overfitting the train set, with smaller train sets needing *more* optimisation. Liu, Michaud, Tegmark ("Omnigrok: Grokking Beyond Algorithmic Data", arXiv:2210.01117, ICLR 2023) attribute it to a mismatch between train and test loss **as functions of weight norm** — the "LU mechanism", train loss L-shaped and test loss U-shaped in ‖θ‖ — which accounts for the data-size and **weight-decay dependence**; they induce grokking on images/language/molecules and *eliminate* it on algorithmic data by changing the initialisation scale. Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt ("Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability", ICLR 2023, arXiv:2301.05217) split training into **memorisation → circuit formation → cleanup**, concluding grokking is gradual mechanism strengthening plus removal of memorising components, not a sudden jump. **Well-established** as phenomena.

**Verdict for your shape: weak fit.** Grokking starts from *chance* generalisation, typically needs small data and (per Omnigrok) weight decay to pull the norm back into the good shell. You have a large dataset, one pass per epoch, and a *good* ep1 metric. **But** Omnigrok's U-shape-in-norm is worth one plot: if val plotted against mean representation norm is U-shaped and your run walks outward past the optimum, that reproduces dip-with-monotone-train-loss, and would recommend norm control (not more epochs). **Plausible, cheap to test.** Note also that your constant-product reparameterisation partly *defeats* the U-shape argument, because the norm can grow without the function's logit scale growing — flag this tension rather than hiding it.

## 1.6 Gradient starvation / shortcut dominance

Pezeshki, Kaba, Bengio, Courville, Precup, Lajoie ("Gradient Starvation: A Learning Proclivity in Neural Networks", NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2011.09468): under cross-entropy, when one feature is learned faster, the gradient contribution of examples containing it is **diminished**, starving the remaining features of signal; they analyse the dynamics and propose Spectral Decoupling as a fix. **Well-established** (analysis + fix, well cited).

**Why it matters here specifically.** Your own project notes record that on at least one dataset an in-degree/popularity-only predictor **beats** the model's MRR, which is exactly the shortcut-dominance configuration. A fast popularity fit would peak val in 1-2 epochs, suppress gradients for relational structure, and the slow tail would be structure finally being learned once the easy term saturates. **Signature:** decompose the score into a popularity component and a residual and track each component's contribution to the metric per epoch; shortcut dominance predicts the popularity component's val contribution peaks early and *flattens or falls* while the residual's rises during the tail. This predicts recovery, so it survives the filter in §1.0.

## 1.7 Adam-specific early-phase behaviour

Liu, Jiang, He, Chen, Liu, Gao, Han ("On the Variance of the Adaptive Learning Rate and Beyond", ICLR 2020, arXiv:1908.03265) show the adaptive LR has **problematically large variance early in training** and that warmup works as variance reduction; RAdam rectifies it analytically. Mosbach, Andriushchenko, Klakow ("On the Stability of Fine-tuning BERT: Misconceptions, Explanations, and Strong Baselines", ICLR 2021, arXiv:2006.04884) find fine-tuning instability is an **optimisation** problem (vanishing gradients early), not catastrophic forgetting or small data; their prescriptions are small LRs, **enabling Adam bias correction**, and **not fixing the epoch count a priori — train for many iterations**. They also note residual variance comes from *generalisation* differences among runs at identical train loss. **Well-established.**

**Fit.** The second-moment warmup window is a few hundred-to-thousand steps. With a large dataset and one pass per epoch, that is a small fraction of epoch 1, so it is unlikely to produce a dip centred at ep4-6. **Weak fit, but cheap to exclude**: rerun with RAdam or 1-2k-step linear warmup and see if the dip changes shape. Note Mosbach's direct relevance to your Part 2(b) question: *do not fix the epoch count in advance*.

## 1.8 Loss–metric mismatch and train/eval distribution mismatch

**Loss–metric mismatch.** Huang, Zhai et al. ("Addressing the Loss-Metric Mismatch with Adaptive Loss Alignment", ICML 2019, PMLR 97:2891-2900, arXiv:1905.05895) name the problem: training losses are chosen for differentiability while the metrics of interest (AUC, precision/recall, ranking metrics) are **non-decomposable and non-continuous**, and their Figure 1 shows train cross-entropy and the evaluation metric differing in both shape and noise, *more so for AUCPR than for accuracy*. **Well-established.**

**The confidence-inflation channel.** Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger ("On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017, PMLR 70, arXiv:1706.04599) document the mechanism concretely: once the model classifies (almost) all training samples correctly, **NLL can be further minimised by increasing the confidence of predictions** — their Figure 3 shows test NLL overfitting while test error continues to improve, with the best-validation-loss and best-validation-error epochs at *different* places. **Well-established, and directly applicable:** your monotone train loss after ep3 may be almost entirely confidence/scale inflation, which your ranking metric cannot see. It also means **you must not select checkpoints on validation loss** — only on the metric.

**Train/eval negative-distribution mismatch.** Your own setup draws training negatives from one pool and evaluation negatives from another (and the suite ships fixed test negatives). Any such mismatch makes the train objective a biased surrogate, and a model can improve on the train negative distribution while degrading on the eval one. I found **no** published treatment of the epoch-wise *shape* this induces — **speculative**, but it is cheap to measure (Part 3) and it is the single most mundane explanation on the list, so rule it out first.

## 1.9 Train/eval-mode discrepancies (normalisation, dropout)

If any BatchNorm or dropout is present, early-epoch eval numbers are systematically unrepresentative because running statistics have not settled, and dropout + BN interact badly: dropout shifts a unit's variance between train and inference while BN keeps the running variance it accumulated, causing "variance shift" and unstable inference (Li, Chen, Hu, Yang, "Understanding the Disharmony Between Dropout and Batch Normalization by Variance Shift", CVPR 2019, pp. 2682-2690, arXiv:1801.05134). **Well-established.** Probably **not applicable** to your model, but it costs one grep to rule out and it is the kind of thing a reviewer will ask.

## 1.10 Loss of plasticity / dormant units

Dohare, Hernandez-Garcia, Lan, Rahman, Mahmood, Sutton ("Loss of plasticity in deep continual learning", *Nature* 632:768-774, 2024, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07711-7) show standard deep learning progressively loses the ability to learn, eventually performing no better than a shallow net, and that a non-gradient (re-initialisation) component is needed to prevent it; Sokar, Agarwal, Castro, Evci ("The Dormant Neuron Phenomenon in Deep Reinforcement Learning", ICML 2023, PMLR 202:32145-32168) characterise the accumulation of inactive units reducing expressivity, with ReDo as the fix. **Well-established** in continual/RL settings; **speculative** for a single-task, 13-epoch supervised run.

**Why keep it on the list anyway.** It is the only mechanism that explains *why the tail might never re-reach the early peak even with unlimited epochs*. If your long run saturates below the early peak, measure unit activity / effective rank before concluding "capacity".

## 1.11 Measurement: winner's curse on the early peak

Selecting the best-scoring candidate on one validation set systematically favours candidates whose score was pushed up by noise, making the reported winner **optimistically biased** and standard intervals invalid (e.g. "A Flexible Defense Against the Winner's Curse", arXiv:2411.18569; "Have we been Naive to Select Machine Learning Models? Noisy Data are here to Stay!", arXiv:2207.06651, which shows a model that fits noisy labels can win on validation while losing on test). Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz, Smith ("Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results", EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019, D19-1224, arXiv:1909.03004) make the budget-dependence of a selected maximum explicit, and show published comparisons whose conclusions flip with budget. **Well-established** as statistics; I found **no paper treating per-epoch checkpoint selection specifically** as an instance, so that step is an extrapolation (**plausible**).

**Fit.** Every epoch is a candidate, so the max-over-13-epochs is upward biased by roughly the metric's epoch-to-epoch noise scale. For your **flat variant** (0.006 total drift) this plausibly explains the *entire* observation — there may be no mechanism to find. For the main variant a 0.069 drop is far outside any reasonable noise band, so the dip there is real.

Restarts help escape, but nothing in the literature says they repair an early-peak-then-dip curve. The honest reason to use SGDR here is its anytime property plus snapshot ensembling/averaging | Cheap. Interacts with early stopping: restarts *guarantee* transient val drops, so the stop rule must be restart-aware |
| **Noise-robust objective** (if false negatives are the driver) | Early-learning regularisation explicitly exploits the two-phase structure to prevent memorisation of noisy labels (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2020); dropout can slow noise-fitting without hurting clean-data generalisation (Arpit et al., ICML 2017); label smoothing is the standard cheap proxy | **Plausible, conditional.** Only worth doing *after* you have measured that the dip scales with negative-sampling noise. ELR itself is a classification method; the ranking analogue is not off-the-shelf | Medium (new loss term); risks underfitting hard negatives |
| **Spectral Decoupling / shortcut mitigation** | Pezeshki et al. (NeurIPS 2021) propose it precisely to decouple feature learning under gradient starvation | **Plausible, conditional** on the popularity-shortcut diagnostic coming back positive | One hyperparameter; evidence base is narrower than the above |
| **Gradient clipping; optimiser swap (RAdam/AdamW/Lion)** | RAdam (Liu et al. ICLR 2020); AdamW-vs-Adam explained by rotation (Kosson et al.) | **Thin** for this shape. Clipping is a stability patch, not a fix for a dip-then-rise; the most defensible swap is Adam→AdamW *because* of the effective-LR argument | Trivial; each swap is a confound unless run as a one-variable A/B |
| **Label smoothing** | Standard regulariser; Guo et al. (ICML 2017) context is that it curbs confidence inflation | **Speculative** here. It attacks the confidence channel, which your *ranking* metric may be indifferent to | Trivial cost, one hyperparameter |

**Honest overall assessment of (a).** Only two interventions have evidence tied to *this* curve rather than to deep learning in general: **Heckel & Yilmaz's per-part step sizes** (which demonstrably eliminates epoch-wise double descent) and **longer training with a decay schedule** (which the double-descent literature predicts will take you past the early peak). The scale-freezing intervention is the one with the strongest *local* evidence — your own constant-product measurement — but the weakest *published* evidence for this exact configuration. Everything below those three is a reasonable hedge, not a prediction.

## (b) Early-stopping and model-selection methodology

The governing principle, and it is the main thing to change: **"stop training" and "select the checkpoint" are two different decisions and must use two different rules.** Patience-on-best is a stopping rule being misused as a selection rule, and that is how you lose a run that is still improving.

| Practice | Why / source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Use a criterion that accounts for training progress, not just "no new best"** | Prechelt, "Early Stopping — But When?", in *Neural Networks: Tricks of the Trade*, Orr & Müller (eds.), Springer LNCS 1524, 1998 (doi:10.1007/3-540-49430-8_3) defines three criterion *classes*: **GL_α** (stop when generalisation loss, the % increase over the best-so-far, exceeds α), **PQ_α** (GL divided by a *training-progress* measure over a strip of k epochs), and **UP_s** (stop after s successive strips of increase). Prechelt's companion study ("Automatic Early Stopping Using Cross Validation: Quantifying the Criteria", *Neural Networks*, 1998) quantifies 14 criteria across 3 classes | **PQ is the one designed for your situation**: its explicit rationale is that *while the training error is still falling quickly, a generalisation loss has a higher chance of being "repaired"*. Your train loss falls 0.43→0.05 monotonically, so PQ would not have stopped at ep13. **Well-established, and under-used.** Note also Prechelt's finding that slower criteria buy small improvements at large time cost |
| **Minimum-epoch floor** | Mosbach et al. (ICLR 2021) slides: *do not fix the number of epochs a priori; train for many iterations*; Nakkiran et al. (ICLR 2020) show the second descent arrives late | Simple and effective. Set the floor from the *observed* location of the trough, not a round number |
| **Patience scaled to measured metric noise, on a smoothed curve** | The winner's-curse literature (arXiv:2411.18569; arXiv:2207.06651) shows that per-epoch maxima on a noisy metric are upward biased; Prechelt's strips are the classical smoothing device | Estimate the noise first (Part 3, D1). Your "+0.0003 val flickers kept resetting patience" note is this failure mode exactly: use **relative-improvement thresholds (min_delta ≫ noise σ)** and require improvement over a *window mean*, not over the running max |
| **Separate best-checkpoint selection from stopping** | Guo et al. (ICML 2017) Fig. 3 shows best-val-*loss* and best-val-*error* epochs differ; Huang et al. (ICML 2019) formalise loss–metric mismatch | Concretely: stop on PQ/plateau of a smoothed metric; select on the metric (never the loss); and keep both **test@val-checkpoint** and **max test** in the record — which your project already does and should keep doing, because ranking arms on `best_test_mrr` alone is not like-for-like |
| **Checkpoint averaging instead of checkpoint picking** | SWA (Izmailov et al., UAI 2018); EMA (Morales-Brotons et al., TMLR 2024); latest-k averaging (Kaddour, arXiv:2209.14981) | Turns "which noisy epoch was best" into a much lower-variance estimate, and removes most of the selection bias. **The cheapest methodology upgrade you can make**, applicable to checkpoints you already have |
| **Multiple validation measurements to estimate noise** | Standard; Dodge et al. (EMNLP 2019) argue for reporting validation-side distributions, not just a selected maximum | Cheap versions: re-draw the eval negatives with k seeds; bootstrap over queries; split val in half and compare. With 17 positives (as in your node-classification lane) the s.e. can be 0.03-0.04 — larger than most effects people report |
| **Report selection-budget-aware numbers** | Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz, Smith (EMNLP 2019, D19-1224): plot *expected* validation performance of the best-found model as a function of budget; they find published conclusions that flip with budget | Directly applicable: "best of 13 epochs" and "best of 100 epochs" are different estimators, so arms with different run lengths are not comparable on a selected max. This is a real hazard in your own tables, where stop epochs range from 4 to 118 |
| **Validation-free stopping as a cross-check** | Mahsereci, Balles, Lassner, Hennig, "Early Stopping without a Validation Set", arXiv:1703.09580 (2017): a stopping rule from local gradient statistics already computed during training, no held-out set needed | Useful as an *independent* second opinion when the metric is noisy and the val set is small. **Plausible** (not widely adopted) |
| **Pitfall to name explicitly in the write-up** | Mosbach et al. (ICLR 2021) report residual variance from models at **identical training loss but noticeably different test performance** | The direct justification for why you cannot use train loss, or train-loss-matched comparisons, to arbitrate between arms |

One caution I could not verify in this session and so flag as such: I believe Bouthillier et al., "Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks" (MLSys 2021), is the standard reference for multi-source variance estimation and for the number of seeds needed; **I did not verify this citation** — check it before using.

# Part 3 — DIAGNOSTICS

Cheapest and most decisive first. Each row says what outcome implies what.

| # | Measurement | Cost | What each outcome implies |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D1** | **Metric noise floor.** Evaluate the *same* checkpoint ~5× with re-drawn eval negatives; and bootstrap the metric over queries | minutes | If σ ≳ 0.005, your flat variant's 0.006 drift is noise and there is nothing to explain; it also fixes `min_delta` and patience for everything else. **Do this before anything else** |
| **D2** | **Sub-epoch validation during ep1-3.** Evaluate every ¼ epoch | small | If the "peak" turns into a still-rising curve, the early peak is a sampling artefact of once-per-epoch eval, and the dip is the first real feature. Resolves whether you even have an early peak |
| **D3** | **Scale-invariance check.** Confirm analytically and numerically that multiplying the whole score by a constant leaves the metric bit-identical; then plot `output_scale`, `mean‖x‖`, their product, *and* the metric per epoch | ~free | If invariant: all loss progress along that direction is metric-invisible ⇒ monotone train loss is uninformative **by construction**, and mechanism 1.3 is live. If *not* invariant (e.g. an additive popularity/bias term breaks it), the drift is moving the metric directly and is the prime suspect |
| **D4** | **Effective step size.** Log per-epoch relative update norm `‖Δθ‖/‖θ‖` (per parameter group: embeddings, pooler, scalar), i.e. Kosson et al.'s rotation proxy | ~free | Monotone decay tracking the dip→rise transition ⇒ the run is self-annealing; the tail is **rate-limited, not capacity-limited**, so longer training / explicit decay should pass the early peak. Flat ⇒ mechanism 1.3 is out |
| **D5** | **Train loss at step granularity, not epoch.** Plus top-Hessian or a cheap power-iteration sharpness proxy if affordable | small / medium | Epoch-level monotonicity can hide the short-timescale non-monotonicity that Cohen et al. show is characteristic of edge-of-stability. If spikes exist, the dip is an instability phenomenon and the fix is LR/warmup, not architecture |
| **D6** | **Shortcut decomposition.** Score a popularity/degree-only predictor; per epoch, report its metric, the model's metric, and the rank correlation between the two | small | High correlation at ep1-3 that *falls* during the tail ⇒ early peak = shortcut, tail = structure, i.e. gradient starvation (1.6) / simplicity bias (1.2). Your own notes already show popularity-only beating the model on one dataset, so the prior is high |
| **D7** | **Noise sweep.** Vary the negative-sampling protocol/hardness (hence false-negative rate) over 3 levels and re-run the short schedule | medium (3 runs) | Dip depth/timing scaling with noise ⇒ epoch-wise double descent in the Stephenson & Lee sense, and noise-robust objectives become the right lever. Dip unchanged ⇒ label noise is **not** the driver, which kills 1.1-noise and 1.2 together |
| **D8** | **Frozen-feature probe.** At ep3, ep6 and ep13, freeze the representation and fit a small/linear head on train; evaluate | small | Probe metric rising monotonically while end-to-end val dips ⇒ **the representation is improving and the readout is misaligned** (the "Two Speeds" decomposition, arXiv:2605.27078) ⇒ fix the head/temperature, not the encoder. Probe metric *also* dipping ⇒ genuine representation degradation |
| **D9** | **Per-example / per-query loss trajectories.** Bucket queries by difficulty; track the loss of each bucket | small | Easy buckets' loss low-and-flat while hard/noisy buckets' loss falls precisely during the dip ⇒ memorisation phase (Arpit et al.; Liu et al.) |
| **D10** | **Checkpoint averaging, retrospectively.** Average the last k checkpoints of runs you already have; evaluate | ~free | If the average beats every individual epoch, most of the "lost" tail was noise around a slowly-moving mean and your reporting problem is largely solved without retraining (Kaddour; Izmailov et al.; Morales-Brotons et al.) |
| **D11** | **Val-metric vs representation-norm plot** (across epochs, and across runs with different init scales) | ~free from logs | A U-shape with the run walking outward reproduces Omnigrok's LU mechanism ⇒ the lever is norm control (weight decay / radius constraint), not epochs |
| **D12** | **Rule-outs.** grep for BatchNorm/dropout; verify train vs eval negative pools; verify Adam bias correction is enabled | minutes | Removes 1.9 and 1.7 from the list, or finds a mundane bug. Do these on day one — the train/eval negative-pool mismatch is the most boring and most likely candidate on the whole list |

**Minimum decisive set, if you only run three things:** D1 + D3/D4 (free, from logging) and the long-run-with-decay experiment named in the summary; then D8 if you need to localise the damage to encoder vs readout.

# What the literature does NOT explain about this shape

Name these gaps rather than papering over them — several are places where you would be extending the literature, not applying it.

1. **Almost all epoch-wise double-descent evidence is test error/loss on image classification with *injected* label noise.** I found no study of epoch-wise double descent in a *ranking* metric (MRR/NDCG/recall@k), nor with sampled-negative "label noise" as the noise source, nor on one-pass-per-epoch large data with Adam. The transfer is a conjecture.
2. **No published account of a first descent completing in 1-3 epochs.** In Nakkiran et al. and Stephenson & Lee the first minimum arrives after tens-to-hundreds of epochs. Your peak-at-ep3-of-13 is outside the regime those papers characterise, and "epoch" is not even the right unit — step count and data passes matter, and the papers' phase diagrams are not parameterised to let you port their thresholds.
3. **Nothing explains a second rise that plateaus *below* the early peak.** The double-descent literature predicts the second descent eventually passes the first minimum; the memorisation literature predicts no recovery at all. "Recovers but never fully" sits between them, and whether that is truncation or irreversible early damage (plasticity loss, which is only studied in continual/RL settings) is, as far as I can tell, **untested**.
4. **The effective-LR / scale-invariance theory is about BN-normalised layers.** van Laarhoven, Li & Arora, Zhang et al., Kodryan et al. and Kosson et al. all assume (near-)exact scale invariance. An embedding table plus a single learned output scalar has *one* degenerate direction, not a per-layer invariance group; whether the regime picture (convergence / chaotic equilibrium) transfers is unverified. This is the biggest leap in my top-3 diagnosis.
5. **Nobody has formalised "metric-invariant loss progress" as a named failure mode**, as far as I found. Guo et al. and Huang et al. get close (confidence inflation; loss–metric mismatch) but neither frames it as *gradient budget spent on a direction with exactly zero metric gradient*, which is what your constant-product observation suggests. If you can measure the fraction of the update lying in the metric-invariant subspace per epoch, that is a small, clean, publishable contribution in its own right.
6. **Checkpoint selection as a winner's-curse instance is not treated in the literature I could find.** The statistics are standard and Dodge et al. cover hyperparameter-search budget, but the epoch-selection case — where candidates are highly *correlated* across epochs, so the usual independent-candidates bias formulas do not apply — I did not find addressed. Treat any bias correction you apply as an estimate.
7. **False-negative noise in sampled negatives is well-known as a problem but not characterised dynamically.** The recommender literature documents the false-negative problem and proposes fixes, but I found no controlled measurement of how false-negative rate shapes the *epoch-wise trajectory* of a ranking metric. That is exactly diagnostic D7, and it appears to be unmeasured in the literature.
